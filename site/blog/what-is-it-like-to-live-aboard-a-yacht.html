What Is It Like to Live Aboard a Yacht in Fort Lauderdale?

Discover what liveaboard life really feels like in Fort Lauderdale — from daily routines and marina costs to the freedom of waking up on the water.

Updated October 12, 2026

Living aboard a yacht in Fort Lauderdale means waking up to water on every side, trading a fixed address for a berth along the Intracoastal Waterway, and folding boat maintenance into the rhythm of daily life. It is quieter than most people expect, more social than many anticipate, and more practical than it sounds — provided you choose the right vessel and the right marina from the start.

What does a typical day look like when you live on a boat?

A liveaboard morning begins with a cup of coffee in the cockpit, often before anyone onshore has stirred. Fort Lauderdale's climate means that ritual is comfortable for most of the year — cool breezes off the water, a sky that turns pink before it turns blue, and the gentle sound of halyards tapping in the distance. From there, the day looks surprisingly ordinary: you commute to work (many Fort Lauderdale liveaboards simply drive or cycle from the marina), run errands, and return home — except home is floating.

Evenings tend to center on the cockpit or the flybridge. Sundowners are a ritual taken seriously in this city, and marina culture here is genuinely neighborly. You will know your dock neighbors far better than most people know theirs. Weekends open up to short cruises down to the Keys, over to the Bahamas, or simply out the inlet and back for a few hours of open water.

How does liveaboard life work in Fort Lauderdale specifically?

Fort Lauderdale is arguably the best city in North America to attempt this lifestyle. The city hosts one of the densest concentrations of marine service providers in the world, which means repairs, provisioning, and technical support are rarely more than a phone call away. The Intracoastal Waterway threads directly through the city, and dozens of full-service marinas — from working boatyards to resort-style facilities with pools and restaurants — line its banks.

Liveaboard slips are available but not unlimited; popular marinas fill up and maintain waiting lists. Choosing your marina is arguably the most consequential early decision you will make. Factors to weigh include:

  • Liveaboard policy: Not all marinas permit full-time habitation; confirm in writing.
  • Shore power capacity: Larger vessels need 50-amp service; verify availability.
  • Security and access: Gated docks, cameras, and controlled entry matter when this is your home.
  • Proximity to amenities: Grocery stores, laundry, and transportation links vary significantly by location.
  • Hurricane preparedness: Ask how the marina handles storm protocols and whether you are required to move the vessel.
  • Community atmosphere: Visit on a weekday and a weekend before you sign anything.

What are the real costs of living aboard a yacht?

The financial picture is more nuanced than the fantasy suggests. Slip fees represent your largest fixed cost and vary considerably by marina tier and vessel length. On top of that, liveaboards budget for electricity (metered separately at most marinas), insurance endorsed for full-time habitation, routine maintenance, and an emergency reserve for unexpected repairs.

The maintenance reality deserves honest discussion. Systems you might defer on a weekend boat — watermaker, generator, air conditioning, holding tank — become load-bearing infrastructure when you live aboard. Fort Lauderdale's heat and humidity are also harder on equipment than most climates. Experienced liveaboards typically set aside a meaningful monthly reserve specifically for vessel upkeep. The upside: Fort Lauderdale's marine trades ecosystem means competitive pricing and skilled technicians, and our maintenance resource hub can help you think through what a sustainable upkeep plan looks like.

It is also worth noting that some people approaching liveaboard life are simultaneously reassessing their broader housing situation — particularly as traditional home costs in South Florida have climbed. For certain buyers, a well-found vessel in a quality marina can represent a meaningful shift in how they think about shelter and lifestyle costs, though individual circumstances vary enormously.

Is the liveaboard lifestyle socially isolating or actually social?

The liveaboard community in Fort Lauderdale is genuinely tight-knit, and most people who try the lifestyle describe becoming more social, not less. Dock communities form around shared problems solved together — a neighbor with the right tool, someone who knows the best local mechanic, a group that paddles out together on Sunday mornings.

Fort Lauderdale also hosts events that draw the broader boating community together year-round, from boat shows to informal raft-ups. If you are considering a charter experience before committing to ownership, spending time aboard gives you a calibrated sense of whether the lifestyle suits you — our charter resource hub is a good place to start that exploration.

What should I know before making the decision to live aboard?

Three things matter above all else before you commit:

  1. Spend meaningful time aboard before buying. A weekend charter and a month living on a boat are completely different experiences. Test the galley, sleep in the berth, try working from the salon.
  2. Choose the vessel for the life you will actually live, not the life you imagine. A bluewater passage-maker sounds romantic; if you will spend 95% of your time at the dock, a well-appointed motoryacht with a large cockpit and robust shore-power systems may serve you better.
  3. Understand your marina options before you buy the boat. In a tight market like Fort Lauderdale, securing a liveaboard-approved slip can take time. Know where you will live before you know what you will live on.

The liveaboard life is not for everyone, but for those it suits, it reshapes what home means in ways that are difficult to fully describe from the outside. Fort Lauderdale — with its year-round warmth, world-class marine infrastructure, and water-first culture — remains one of the few places where living this way is not an eccentric choice but a well-worn path.

FAQ

Quick answers.

Yes, living aboard a yacht is legal in Fort Lauderdale, though individual marinas set their own liveaboard policies and some limit the number of permitted liveaboard slips. Always confirm liveaboard status directly with the marina before committing to a berth.
Slip costs vary widely depending on vessel length, marina amenities, and location along the Intracoastal Waterway. Liveaboard slips typically carry a premium over standard transient or seasonal rates, and some marinas add a liveaboard surcharge on top of the base slip fee.
Most marina slips include shore power connections, and liveaboards pay for electricity based on usage — often metered separately. Fresh water, Wi-Fi, pump-out services, and laundry facilities may be bundled into slip fees or billed individually depending on the marina.
VA-guaranteed home loan benefits generally apply to traditional real estate, not vessels, so a liveaboard yacht purchase typically falls outside that program's scope. Eligible veterans should consult a qualified advisor to understand which purchase options are available to them.
Managing maintenance is consistently cited as the most demanding aspect of liveaboard life — systems that would be optional on a weekend boat become critical when you live aboard full time. Building a reliable relationship with a local marine service team is essential, and Fort Lauderdale's deep concentration of marine trades makes that easier than almost anywhere else.
Standard boat insurance policies often exclude full-time habitation, so liveaboards typically need a policy specifically endorsed for continuous occupancy. Coverage should address liability, personal property aboard, and hurricane preparedness given South Florida's storm season.
Most experienced liveaboards recommend a minimum of around 35 feet for a single person and 40 to 45 feet or more for a couple, to allow for comfortable storage, a proper galley, and a dedicated sleeping cabin. Larger vessels offer more comfort but come with proportionally higher operating and slip costs.
