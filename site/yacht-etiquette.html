Yacht Etiquette: What Everyone Aboard Expects

Most yachting customs exist for good reason. Here is what makes you a welcome guest and a competent host, without memorizing a rulebook.

Yachting has its own customs, and most of them exist for good reason — they come from safety, from close quarters, or from centuries of tradition at sea. Whether you're joining friends for a day, boarding a charter, or buying your first boat, understanding yacht etiquette makes you a welcome guest and a competent host. None of it is difficult. Most of it is simply knowing what people expect.

Being a good guest aboard

The single most important rule: the captain's word is final. This isn't formality — at sea, someone must have unambiguous authority, and safety depends on it. If the captain asks you to do something, do it without debate. If they say the plan has changed because of weather, that is the end of the discussion.

Beyond that, a few basics cover most situations. Ask before boarding — stepping onto someone's yacht uninvited is a genuine breach. Remove your shoes unless told otherwise; deck surfaces mark easily and hard soles do real damage. Pack in a soft bag, since hard suitcases won't fit in yacht storage. Bring less than you think, because space aboard is genuinely limited.

Once aboard, be conscious of water and power, both of which are finite. Long showers are a real imposition on a vessel making its own water. Ask before using equipment you're unfamiliar with. And keep your things in your cabin — shared spaces stay shared.

Safety customs

Some conventions exist purely because the sea is unforgiving. Listen to the safety briefing properly, even if you've heard one before, since every vessel is different and knowing where things are matters only when something has gone wrong. Tell someone if you go on deck at night or in rough conditions. Don't sit on the bow underway unless the captain has said it's fine.

Learn where the life jackets, fire extinguishers, and first aid kit are on your first day aboard. And if you notice something that seems wrong — an unusual sound, water where it shouldn't be, an odd smell — say so immediately. Crew would far rather investigate ten false alarms than miss one real problem.

Dealing with crew

On a crewed yacht, the crew are skilled professionals, and treating them as such produces a far better experience for everyone. Be polite and clear about what you'd like; they are genuinely there to make your trip good, and specific requests are easier to fulfil than hints. Respect their working areas — the galley and crew quarters are their space, not part of the tour.

On charter, gratuity is customary, typically in the range of ten to twenty percent of the base charter fee, given at the end and shared among the crew. Communicate dietary needs and preferences early rather than at the table, since provisioning happens before departure and a chef can't produce what wasn't brought aboard.

Harbour and marina manners

Marinas are dense communities, and the customs reflect that. Keep noise down, particularly early and late — sound carries remarkably well over water, and your music is everyone's music. Don't walk across someone else's boat to reach your own without asking. Coil and stow your lines rather than leaving them across the dock.

Help with lines when a neighbour is docking; it's expected, appreciated, and reciprocal. Keep your dock space tidy. And observe wake discipline — moving too fast through a mooring field or marina throws boats around, spills drinks, and marks you immediately as someone who doesn't know better.

Flags and tradition

Yachting retains genuine maritime tradition, and flag customs are the most visible part. The ensign, showing the vessel's nationality, is flown at the stern and traditionally raised at eight in the morning and lowered at sunset. A courtesy flag of the host country is flown when visiting foreign waters — a real diplomatic courtesy, not decoration. Yacht clubs have their own burgees, flown to show affiliation.

These conventions matter more in some circles than others, but getting them right is noticed by those who care, and getting them wrong occasionally causes genuine offence when visiting other countries.

Hosting well

If you're the one with the boat, a few things make you a good host. Brief your guests before departure — safety equipment, the head, water conservation, and what to expect from the day. Be clear about the plan, including when you'll return, since guests can't easily leave partway through.

Consider seasickness in advance; ask whether anyone is prone to it and have remedies aboard, since a guest suffering quietly is a miserable experience for them and awkward for everyone. Provide more water and shade than you think you need. And accept that as skipper you are working — you'll be less relaxed than your guests, which is simply part of the role.

The bottom line

Yacht etiquette comes down to respecting the captain's authority, taking safety seriously, treating crew as professionals, being conscious of finite resources, and observing the courtesies that make close-quarters and marina life work. None of it requires memorizing rules. Most of it follows from paying attention and being considerate — which is, in fairness, most etiquette anywhere.

Etiquette specific to charter

Chartering adds a few conventions worth knowing, since you are simultaneously a guest and a paying client. Communicate preferences early and clearly — the crew genuinely want to tailor the trip, but they need to know what you want before provisioning and planning are complete. A preference sheet is usually sent in advance, and filling it in properly makes an enormous difference to the experience.

Understand the APA arrangement, where an advance allowance covers fuel, food, dockage, and incidentals, with the captain accounting for spending as the trip progresses. Ask about the balance if you're curious rather than being surprised at the end. Be reasonable about itinerary changes: crew will accommodate what they can, but weather, port availability, and cruising distances impose real constraints that are not negotiable.

And remember that the crew are working long hours in confined quarters for the duration of your trip. Guests who are pleasant, clear, and reasonable get noticeably better service than those who treat the arrangement as licence to be demanding — and the difference shows up in a hundred small ways over a week aboard.
