How Much Does a Yacht Cost? The Complete 2026 Breakdown

The purchase price is only the beginning. This guide breaks down the real yacht cost — from sticker prices by size to the ongoing cost of ownership that catches first-time buyers off guard.

If you ask a broker how much a yacht costs, you'll get a purchase price — and that's the wrong number to anchor on. The true yacht cost is what it takes to own her: purchase, plus the running costs that recur every single year. Understanding the full picture is the difference between yacht ownership being one of life's great joys and one of its great regrets.

This guide gives you the honest, complete answer. We'll walk through yacht prices by size, the single most useful rule of thumb in yachting, and every major ownership cost — maintenance, dockage, fuel, crew, and insurance. By the end, you'll know not just what a yacht costs to buy, but what it costs to keep.

How much does a yacht cost to buy?

The word "yacht" spans an enormous range, and yacht prices reflect that. A modest, well-kept cruising yacht can cost about the same as a luxury car. A new mid-size motor yacht runs into the millions. And the superyachts that define harbors like Fort Lauderdale and Monaco start in the tens of millions and climb past nine figures. Here's the lay of the land:

Yacht type & sizeTypical price range

These ranges are deliberately broad, because luxury yacht price depends on far more than length. Builder reputation, age, condition, refit history, and equipment all move the number dramatically. A twenty-year-old yacht from a legendary yard can command more than a new boat from a budget builder. A super yacht price from a top European shipyard reflects bespoke craftsmanship that mass-produced vessels simply can't match. So treat the table as orientation, not gospel — and read on, because the purchase price is the part that matters least over the life of ownership.

The 10% rule: the most important number in yachting

Here's the figure that matters more than the sticker price. The widely-cited rule of thumb among experienced owners is that annual running costs equal roughly 10% of the yacht's value, every year.

The 10% Rule

Budget for yearly ownership costs of about 10% of the purchase price. A $2 million yacht costs roughly $200,000 a year to run — before you've gone anywhere special.

This isn't a precise law. A meticulously maintained smaller yacht you captain yourself can come in well under 10%, while a large yacht with full-time crew and heavy use can exceed it. But as a planning figure, it's remarkably durable — and it's the best gut-check there is before you buy. If 10% of a yacht's price makes you flinch, that's your signal you're looking at too much boat. The happiest owners plan for the running costs first and let that set their purchase budget — not the other way around.

Where the annual yacht cost actually goes

That yearly figure isn't a single bill — it's a stack of recurring costs. On a typical mid-size yacht, the major categories are maintenance, dockage, fuel, crew, and insurance. Let's take each in turn, because knowing where the money goes is how you control it.

Yacht maintenance costs

A yacht is a complex machine sitting in saltwater — about the most hostile environment there is for one. That makes yacht maintenance cost a constant, not an occasional, expense. Routine upkeep covers engine servicing, systems checks, cleaning and detailing, and the steady replacement of parts that corrode or wear. Periodically the yacht comes out of the water entirely for a haul-out: bottom paint, through-hull service, and whatever the survey turns up.

As a rough planning figure, maintenance alone often runs in the range of 1–3% of the yacht's value annually for a well-kept vessel — more if she's older or has been neglected. Deferring maintenance is the single fastest way to turn a bargain purchase into a money pit, which is why experienced owners treat it as non-negotiable. We cover this in depth in our guide to yacht maintenance costs.

Dockage and marina fees

Where you keep the yacht is one of the largest recurring costs, and it varies enormously by location. Marinas typically charge per foot of length per month, and prime locations — like the deep-water marinas of Fort Lauderdale, the Yachting Capital of the World — command a premium. For a larger yacht in a desirable marina, dockage alone can run into tens of thousands of dollars a year.

This is also where the line between yachting and real estate blurs. Some owners buy a waterfront home with a private dock, or a dockominium (a dock slip you own outright), rather than paying marina fees indefinitely. For frequent users, owning your dockage can change the long-term math considerably — a point we explore in our guide to dockage and marina costs.

Fuel costs

Yachts are not efficient, and fuel is the cost most directly tied to how you actually use the boat. A yacht that mostly sits at the dock and cruises locally burns relatively little; one that crosses oceans burns a fortune. A single fill-up on a large motor yacht can be staggering — which is why the question "how much is a gallon of gas for a yacht" misses the point. The real driver is consumption: large engines moving a heavy hull through water consume fuel at rates that dwarf any car. Sailing yachts, by contrast, can cruise for next to nothing under sail — one of the quiet economic advantages of canvas over diesel.

Crew costs

Below a certain size, you run the yacht yourself. Above it — generally somewhere around 70–80 feet — you need professional crew, and crew salaries become the single largest line item on a large yacht. At minimum that means a captain; on bigger vessels, a full team of engineer, mate, stewardess, and chef. This is precisely why the 10% rule scales the way it does: crew costs grow steeply with size. An owner-operated 45-foot yacht and a fully-crewed 150-foot superyacht are different financial universes, even though both are "yachts." We break down the numbers in our guide to yacht crew costs and salaries.

Insurance and the unexpected

Yacht insurance scales with the vessel's value, where and how you cruise, and your experience. Beyond insurance, prudent owners keep a contingency reserve — because saltwater, weather, and complex systems guarantee that something, eventually, will need attention you didn't plan for. Building that reserve into your budget is what separates relaxed owners from stressed ones.

What's the cheapest way into yacht ownership?

If you're drawn to the lifestyle but the numbers above feel daunting, there are real ways in at the lower end. The cheapest yachts tend to be older, smaller, well-maintained vessels in the 30–40 foot range — boats where you can handle much of the upkeep yourself and run without crew. Sailing yachts are often more economical to own than equivalent motor yachts, thanks to minimal fuel costs. And buying used lets someone else absorb the steep early depreciation. The key is honesty about the running costs: a cheap purchase price with running costs you can't sustain is no bargain at all.

Should you finance or pay cash?

Most buyers don't pay all cash — and the sophisticated ones often choose not to, even when they could. Financing a yacht works much like financing a home: the loan is secured by the vessel, terms run long, and keeping your capital liquid lets it work elsewhere while you spread the cost over the years you actually enjoy the boat. For many buyers, that's the smarter structure, not the fallback. The full picture — down payments, terms, and the cash-versus-finance tradeoff — is covered in our guide to financing a yacht.

The bottom line on yacht cost

A yacht costs far more than its price tag — but in a predictable, plannable way. The owners who are happy years later are the ones who bought with the full picture in view: who planned for the 10%, not just the purchase price. Go in knowing the real cost of ownership, and yachting is among the great joys there is. Go in with only the sticker price, and the water gets expensive fast. Plan for the whole number, buy the right amount of boat, and the Yachting Capital of the World is yours to enjoy.
