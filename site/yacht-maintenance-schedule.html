Yacht Maintenance Schedule

Most maintenance failures come from not having a system. Here is a practical schedule you can adapt to your vessel and actually follow.

Most maintenance failures aren't caused by ignorance — they're caused by not having a system. Owners know the anodes need checking; they just don't remember when they last did it. A written yacht maintenance schedule turns a vague sense of obligation into a set of specific tasks with dates attached, and that shift alone prevents most of what goes wrong. Here's a practical framework you can adapt to your vessel.

Building your schedule

Your schedule starts with two documents: the builder's manual and the engine manufacturer's service schedule. These specify intervals for your particular vessel, and they override any general advice — including this page. What a general framework adds is structure for everything the manuals don't cover, and a rhythm you can actually keep.

Build it around two clocks. Calendar time governs corrosion, UV, growth, and expiry dates. Engine hours govern mechanical wear. Most items are due at whichever comes first, which is why tracking hours matters even for a lightly used boat.

Weekly (or every visit)

These take minutes and catch problems early:

  • Check the bilges — water where there shouldn't be any is the earliest warning of many problems
  • Confirm bilge pumps and float switches operate
  • Inspect dock lines, fenders, and chafe points
  • Verify shore power connection and check for heat at the plug
  • Look over the deck and cockpit for anything out of place
  • Rinse salt off after any use

Monthly

  • Wash the hull and topsides properly
  • Check engine oil and coolant levels
  • Inspect and clean sea strainers
  • Test all electronics and navigation equipment
  • Check battery condition and charging system operation
  • Run the engines and generator if the yacht hasn't been used
  • Inspect anodes if accessible
  • Check for early signs of mildew in enclosed spaces

Quarterly and seasonal

  • Wax and polish exterior surfaces; treat brightwork
  • Service or replace fuel filters and water separators
  • Inspect belts, hoses, and clamps for wear or softening
  • Check steering and throttle controls for smooth operation
  • Test all safety equipment and check expiry dates
  • Inspect canvas, covers, and upholstery
  • On sailing yachts, inspect running rigging, sails, and winches
  • Deep clean the interior and check for moisture intrusion

Annual

  • Full engine and generator service per the manufacturer's schedule
  • Impeller replacement and cooling system inspection
  • Complete electrical system check, including battery replacement assessment
  • Professional rigging inspection on sailing yachts
  • Liferaft servicing and safety equipment certification
  • Insurance survey if required by your policy
  • Review and update maintenance records

Every one to two years: the haul-out

  • Pressure wash and inspect the bottom
  • Apply antifouling paint
  • Replace all anodes
  • Service through-hulls and seacocks
  • Inspect running gear — propellers, shafts, struts, rudders, and cutless bearings
  • Check hull for blisters, damage, or osmosis
  • Address anything the inspection reveals while access is easy

The haul-out is the anchor of the whole schedule, and the one item never to defer. Everything below the waterline is invisible until the yacht comes out, which means problems there grow unobserved. Our yacht maintenance guide explains why this event carries so much weight.

Tracking it

A schedule you don't record isn't a schedule. Keep a log — spreadsheet, notebook, or app — recording each task with its date and the engine hours at the time. This does three things: it tells you what's due, it reveals patterns when something recurs, and it becomes the service history that supports your yacht's value at sale.

Set calendar reminders for the annual and haul-out items, since those are far enough apart to slip. The weekly and monthly work tends to happen naturally once you're in the habit of a quick walkthrough every time you're aboard.

The bottom line

Adapt this framework to your vessel using the builder's and engine manufacturer's schedules, track both calendar time and engine hours, and record everything as you go. The specific intervals matter less than having a system you actually follow. Owners who work from a written schedule spend less, break down less, and hand a buyer a documented history when the time comes. It is the least glamorous part of ownership and among the most valuable.

Adapting the schedule to your climate

A generic schedule needs adjusting for where the yacht actually lives, because climate changes which items matter most. In warm saltwater like South Florida, marine growth is aggressive year-round, so bottom cleaning between haul-outs becomes routine rather than optional, and antifouling wears faster. Intense UV degrades gelcoat, canvas, and upholstery quickly, making waxing and covers more important than they would be further north. Humidity encourages mildew, so ventilation and interior drying deserve regular attention. And hurricane season adds its own calendar item: a documented storm plan, checked lines and fenders, and decisions about whether to haul or relocate.

In colder climates, winterization dominates the annual cycle — draining or protecting water systems, stabilizing fuel, protecting engines from freezing, and proper covering. The trade-off is that growth and UV are far less aggressive, so bottom and cosmetic intervals stretch.

In freshwater, corrosion is dramatically reduced and anodes last considerably longer, though growth still occurs and mechanical servicing is unchanged. Wherever you are, the principle is the same: identify which environmental pressures are strongest where your yacht sits, and weight the schedule toward those.

Scheduling mistakes owners make

A few patterns account for most schedule failures. Front-loading everything into spring creates a crush of work that competes with the season and with every other owner doing the same thing — spreading tasks through the year works far better. Tracking only calendar time misses engine-hour-based servicing on a heavily used boat, while tracking only hours misses corrosion and expiry items on a lightly used one.

Not recording as you go is the most common of all, and it quietly undermines everything else — a schedule you can't verify against a log is guesswork. And deferring the haul-out to save money in a given year is the single most expensive scheduling decision an owner can make, since it postpones inspection of the systems least visible and most consequential.

Adjusting for how much you actually use the yacht

A schedule built for a boat running two hundred hours a year fits badly on one running twenty, and vice versa. Heavy-use vessels need engine servicing driven primarily by hours, more frequent filter changes, faster wear on impellers and belts, and closer attention to running gear. The calendar items still apply, but the mechanical clock runs much faster.

Lightly used vessels face the opposite pattern, and owners often under-maintain them by assuming that low hours mean low need. In reality, fuel degrades, seals dry out, batteries discharge, systems seize from disuse, and mildew flourishes in a closed interior. For these boats, calendar-driven tasks dominate, and one of the most valuable habits is simply running the engines and generator regularly and moving the vessel occasionally, even with no destination in mind. A boat that never moves is a boat quietly deteriorating.
